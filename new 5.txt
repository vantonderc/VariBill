The core rule: No landlord can evict you, lock you out, cut services, or remove your belongings without a court order. This is a constitutional protection, not just a rental-law nicety — the Constitution provides that no one's property may be taken away and no one may be evicted from their home without a court order, which is why the process runs through the Prevention of Illegal Eviction from and Unlawful Occupation of Land Act (PIE).
How the process actually works, step by step:

Cancel the right to occupy. The landlord must first cancel or withdraw whatever consent or right they gave you to occupy — e.g. cancelling the lease because of a breach like unpaid rent. Once that happens, you become an "unlawful occupier" in the technical legal sense — this is just a label used in the Act, not a moral judgment.
Notice and court papers. Sections 4(1)–(5) of PIE lay down mandatory procedural steps, including that the court must serve written and effective notice of the eviction proceedings on the occupier at least 14 days before the hearing. Theppra
The court hearing. A magistrate decides whether eviction is "just and equitable." If you've occupied for less than six months, the court specifically weighs circumstances like the rights of elderly people, children, disabled persons, and female-headed households. Longer occupation (like your two years) generally gets even more weight in your favour.
Enforcement. Only after a court order is granted can enforcement happen. If you don't vacate by the date in the order, the clerk of court issues a warrant of ejectment, and only then is the Sheriff instructed to remove you — using a locksmith if necessary. A landlord doing any of this personally (changing locks, cutting power, throwing out possessions) is acting unlawfully. Theppra

On your verbal/unwritten lease: that's legally valid in SA — it's just governed by common law and the Rental Housing Act rather than a written contract's specific clauses. A tenancy with no fixed term defaults to month-to-month, cancellable with one calendar month's notice running from the 1st to the last day of a month (notice given mid-month rolls the effective date to the start of the next month).

1. Painters/workers sleeping in the house without your consent
Your right to privacy and undisturbed use of the property is a legal principle in South Africa (often referred to as commodus usus), and it's reinforced by the Rental Housing Act's protection against unwarranted search and seizure, and your right to security of your possessions. Bringing strangers to sleep inside your home — even the landlord's own workers — without your explicit agreement is not something a landlord can unilaterally impose. Even routine access for repairs generally requires notice of around 24 hours except in genuine emergencies. Overnight lodging of workers goes well beyond that and isn't a right the landlord has at all, consented to or not.
2. Landlord's disruption interfering with your ability to earn income / interviews
There isn't a specific "right to earn income" clause in SA rental law, but this connects to two things:

Quiet enjoyment / peaceful occupation — you're entitled to peaceful occupation of the rented property, and disturbances without valid reason breach that right.
Harassment as unfair practice — the Rental Housing Tribunal's mandate explicitly covers harassment and intimidation as complaint categories. Persistent disruptive conduct that materially interferes with your life (job hunting, interviews, work) can be argued as part of a harassment/unfair-practice complaint, even though "lost income from a missed interview" isn't a named statutory head of damages — it's evidence supporting how unreasonable and damaging the conduct is.

3. Illegal eviction (the core protection)
Constitutionally, no one may be evicted from their home without a court order — this is bedrock. The PIE Act sets out the mandatory process: the landlord must first validly cancel your right to occupy, then the court must serve written notice at least 14 days before any hearing, and only after a court grants an order can the Sheriff — not the landlord — remove you. Locking you out, cutting utilities, removing belongings, or any form of self-help eviction is unlawful regardless of what you owe or how long you've overstayed a notice.
4. Fear of homelessness
This is where PIE goes further than people expect — courts are constitutionally required to weigh your personal circumstances (including whether eviction would leave you homeless) and set a "just and equitable" date, and can factor in the need for alternative accommodation before granting removal. The 2026 PIE Amendment Bill currently before Parliament proposes to formalize this further — making relevant state bodies responsible for ensuring alternative accommodation is arranged fairly and with dignity where an eviction order requires it, and letting courts set the terms of any temporary housing. That bill isn't law yet, but it signals where the courts already lean under the existing Act.
5. Costs incurred (fuel/money to access your stored belongings)
